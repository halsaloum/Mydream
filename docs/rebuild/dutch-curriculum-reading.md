## Progression and interaction

Start with word occurrence, word form and lexeme; use families and paradigms to explain why one visible form can have several grammatical jobs. Establish classes through converging distribution and inflection evidence, rather than semantic slogans such as “a verb is an action.” Move from the finite verb to whole constituents; distinguish phrase category, sentence function and semantic role. Reuse a test, then remove support on a different sentence.

Next teach heads and nested structure, subject agreement, argument versus adjunct, predication, active/passive alternatives, Dutch V2 and verb brackets, coordination and embedded finite/nonfinite clauses, relative attachment, pronoun reference, information structure, scope and implied meanings. Word structure belongs alongside phrase structure, with separate notation and separate evidence. End by producing a full sentence from given propositions, explaining grammatical, semantic and reader-facing choices.

Each lesson needs a discovery question, substantial explanation, annotated examples, a counterexample or test limitation, a manipulable experiment, mixed independent practice, own-text transfer with a rubric, delayed retrieval, and chapter-specific source references. Immediate answer display is avoided until an attempt; self-assessed writing is labeled as such. Progress comes from actual saved attempts and first responses, not sample scores.

## Corrections to carry into new material

- Count V2 by constituent, not word. A sentence with an embedded finite clause has more than one finite verb; a nonfinite clause has none. Do not equate number of finite verbs with all possible clause types.
- Preserve the distinction between a phrase and a sentence-level constituent. Nested phrases do not all independently fill the first field.
- Constituent tests yield converging evidence. Their constructions have syntactic and pragmatic requirements; failure does not prove nonconstituency, and apparent coordination may involve ellipsis.
- Subject agreement should follow the structural head, with construction-specific variation. Do not make proximity an unconditional rule for all disjunctive subjects.
- A passive test is useful evidence for objects, not a universal decision procedure. Some transitive expressions resist ordinary passive use. Optional realization does not prove adjuncthood.
- Copular verbs must be analyzed in context. Place predication and the boundaries of school terminology require careful examples.
- In ordinary Dutch embedded clauses, verbs form the second pole; the finite verb need not be the final individual verb. Both auxiliary–participle and participle–auxiliary orders exist.
- A nonfinite clause may have subject control or object control: “Noor vroeg Mila te vertrekken” is not governed by a blanket “subject always matches main-clause subject” rule.
- Hen/hun are person references in the relevant standard usage; use ze for things. De/het alone does not completely determine personal pronoun gender in all varieties.
- Context may resolve several antecedent candidates. Explicit reference is often useful in formal writing, but “one candidate exactly” is a clarity target, not a universal grammar law.
- Presupposition diagnostics are tendencies; projection and cancellation depend on context. Do not label every adjective or relative clause a presupposition trigger.
- Do not require invariant ceilings of 12 bracket words, 3 verbs, 3 modifiers or 3 finite clauses. Treat counts as prompts for reader-oriented revision, without invented percentages or guaranteed effects.
- Nominalization and passive voice have legitimate discourse functions. Ask what becomes the topic, what information is preserved and who needs to be named.
- Semantic composition also uses constituent structure. The supplied mockup's claim that semantics has no use for trees is too broad.
- Transfer and retrieval are complementary. Notation, colorful symbols and a completed quiz do not establish durable mastery.
