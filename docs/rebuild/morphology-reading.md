## Reading notes accumulated by chapter

### Lieber 1–2: morphology as reasoning, lexemes and mental lexicon

Start with learners' own ability to interpret or create a new word, then identify systematic form–meaning patterns. A written space is not a universal definition of a word. Keep a clean distinction between token (one occurrence), type (one distinct form) and lexeme (the family of grammatical forms). Derivation creates a new lexeme; inflection expresses grammatical information within one lexeme. More inflection is not a more expressive or more advanced language.

A printed dictionary is selective and shaped by its purpose, while a speaker's mental lexicon contains phonological, semantic, grammatical and contextual knowledge. Absence from a dictionary does not establish impossibility. Productive forms can be understood without being memorized as wholes. Evidence supports interactions between storage and construction, with lexical and family frequency mattering; do not claim that all irregulars are stored and all regulars always recomputed. Research on acquisition, aphasia and brain imaging has competing interpretations and methodological limits.

App consequences: use invented but interpretable Dutch items to test transfer; contrast `loop`, `loopt`, `liep` with `loper`; invite a learner to explain a new word's likely meaning rather than judging dictionary membership. Provide a running word notebook as a discovery extension. Avoid reusing dated word-count estimates, clinical claims or lexicographic statistics as timeless facts.

### Lieber 3: structure, word formation and evidence

Free/bound and root/base/stem are distinct dimensions; a bound root is not automatically an affix. Affix rules encode eligible input category, resulting category, meaning and phonological or semantic restrictions. Internal word structure is hierarchical rather than a flat string of pieces: attachment order changes what base an affix sees and can produce ambiguity. Meanings can be compositional or lexicalized; affix polysemy may connect participant and instrument readings. Morphological segmentation can be uncertain: recurrent fragments can lack independent modern meanings; interfixes do not have to be plural suffixes; sound symbolism alone does not make a morpheme.

Compounds combine lexical bases and can be classified independently by category, semantic relationship and headedness. The head determines a word's category and general meaning, but exocentric and coordinative compounds complicate simple rules. Compounding is recursive. Orthography and stress are useful clues with exceptions, not universal decisive tests. Conversion changes category without a derivational segment; Dutch infinitive `-en` is inflection, so it does not by itself prove that a noun-to-verb pair was formed by a derivational suffix. Blending, clipping, backformation, acronyms and coinages are distinct from ordinary affixation.

App consequences: use `on-lees-baar` versus `[on-[lees-baar]]`, `fietsbel` versus `belfiets` and `schoolboekenkast` for bracketing and head tests. Treat the letter sequence in `winkel` as no evidence for a diminutive or suffix. Use `tafel` / `tafeltje`, `werk` / `werker`, `vader` / `vaderschap` to expose multiple word-family relationships. Offer uncertainty as an answer when multiple analyses have evidence.

### Lieber 4: productivity and creativity

Productivity is a gradient capacity to form new lexemes, affected by transparency, eligible bases, usefulness and grammatical, phonological, semantic, pragmatic and historical restrictions. An existing suffix may be recognizable while no longer productive. Lexicalized high-frequency outputs do not establish strong current productivity. Type counts, token counts and hapaxes answer different questions; corpus size, genre and cleaning affect any comparison. Baayen's potential-productivity measure uses hapax count divided by total tokens, with interpretation requiring comparable samples. Productive automatic extension and deliberately salient creativity have fuzzy boundaries.

App consequences: ask learners to generate a plausible new `-baar` or compound word, then justify the base and interpretation. Contrast knowledge of an existing word with willingness to form a new one. A tiny classroom word list cannot establish language-wide productivity. Keep corpus-based experiments as advanced extensions.

### Lieber 5–7: broader mechanisms, inflection and typology

Morphological analysis is a hypothesis about systematic pairings of form and meaning, revised against further contrasts; do not segment based on one pair alone. Processes include infixation, circumfixation, stem alternations, reduplication, templates and subtraction. Several prefixes preceding a root are not infixes: an infix interrupts a root. Dutch `gebergte` illustrates a collective circumfix, while Dutch past participles require more careful lexical and syntactic treatment than a universal “ge-...-t rule”. Derivation and inflection can use the same formal operation; function, not visible shape, distinguishes them.

Inflectional distinctions may include number, person, noun class, case, tense, aspect, voice and modality. Keep form and function apart: periphrastic tense/aspect expressions use several words, while a single bound segment can combine several features. Gender class is not identical to biological sex. Person is a discourse relation (speaker, addressee, other), not simply a word's position. Tense locates an event relative to a reference point; aspect concerns its internal temporal view. The perfect is not synonymous with the perfective. Voice organizes grammatical realization of participants; it does not change who physically acted.

A paradigm groups forms of a lexeme by grammatical cell. Inflection classes differ in form patterns. Syncretism = one surface form across several cells; suppletion = distinctly unrelated stems within a lexeme; overabundance = alternative forms in a cell; defectiveness = an absent cell. Inherent features and features determined by context must be distinguished. The derivation/inflection boundary sometimes forms a continuum, so an introductory rule is a prototype rather than an exceptionless universal.

Typology compares independent dimensions (morphemes per word, separability, feature exponence, head/dependent marking); whole languages are rarely pure members of one of four traditional types. Morphological richness does not measure expressive power, intelligence or grammatical quality. Genetic family and areal contact may influence shared patterns, and languages change typologically over time. For this Dutch curriculum, contrast Dutch locally with a small, carefully glossed dataset rather than teaching elaborate comparative taxonomy before sentence analysis.


### Lieber 8–11: sentence interface, sounds and competing accounts

Verb valency concerns participants selected by a predicate; an unspoken object can still be an argument, so the deletion test alone does not define argumenthood. Adjuncts add contextual circumstances. Passive backgrounds an agent and changes grammatical realization; causatives add a causer; applicatives introduce a promoted object. Separable Dutch verbs, clitics and phrasal compounds sit at the morphology–syntax interface. Keep a finite verb, an infinitive and a lexical head distinct when describing Dutch word order: the book's deliberately simple main-clause examples do not justify “the main verb is always second” for sentences with auxiliaries.

Allomorphs are alternative forms of a morpheme selected by a context. Distinguish phonologically predictable patterns from lexically specified ones. Surface spelling is not a complete account of sound patterns. Dutch diminutive forms `-je`, `-tje`, `-etje`, `-pje`, `-kje` are a useful local lab: word-final sounds, vowel length and stress can matter. Start from observable contrasts rather than memorizing a table in isolation. `hond` versus `honden` lets learners observe final devoicing while preserving the morphological family. Phonological syllables and meaningful morphemes are different units.

Theory chapter distinctions prevent dogmatism in the app. Item-and-arrangement, item-and-process and word-and-paradigm models differ in units and predictions; nonconcatenative morphology and multiple exponence complicate a bead-like segment model. Lexical integrity, blocking and affix-order restrictions are hypotheses with counterexamples, not inviolable facts. Distributed Morphology, Construction Morphology, Paradigm Function Morphology, Natural Morphology, Naive Discriminative Learning and Lexical Semantic Framework make different commitments and address different data. A school-level curriculum should teach empirical contrasts and justified analyses, while placing theoretical comparison in optional depth panels. Treat `-er` interpretations as a related meaning cluster and explain that a subject need not be an agent.


### Haspelmath/Sims 1–5 (printed1–113)

Chapter 1 frames morphology as systematic covariation of form and meaning and distinguishes descriptive elegance from cognitive realism and explanatory goals. Typological scales cannot rank languages. Chapter 2 carefully separates word-token, abstract lexeme, word-form (including grammatical feature specification) and larger word family. A paradigm can contain more grammatical cells than distinct strings. The base is relative to a process, the root is the irreducible lexical core, and a stem may be bound. Allomorph type (phonological/weakly suppletive/strongly suppletive) and conditioning (sound, grammatical environment, lexical identity) are different dimensions.

Chapters 3–4 motivate word-based correspondence alongside segment-based analysis. Base modification, conversion, backformation and cross-formation cannot all be taught as “add a suffix”. Zero expression does not require an audible zero token; empty morphs do not carry transparent lexical meanings. Complete words, bases and schemas can coexist in lexical representations; processing patterns do not prove a single exclusive storage route. Dutch `gesproken` versus `besproken` directly illustrates how morphology affects participle formation. The authors treat clippings and acronyms outside strict morphology whereas Lieber surveys them among minor formation processes: teach this as a difference in disciplinary scope, not a contradiction to test as one correct answer.

Chapter 5 tests eleven proposed inflection/derivation diagnostics and emphasizes prototypes and the continuum/dichotomy debate. Especially important for Dutch: derivation can preserve category (`kind` → `kindje`) while changing lexical meaning; contextual agreement differs from inherent number/tense; lexicalized forms can gain a separate meaning (`eten` as noun versus infinitive). A grammatical category is not guaranteed to have one visible ending. Use several converging criteria; do not make category change the sole diagnostic. Inherent and contextual inflection can share a cumulative exponent, so simplistic “first create word then always add inflection” explanations have limits.



## Cumulative notes: Haspelmath & Sims chapters 6–12

All twelve chapters and the complete backmatter were read before authoring; the final ledger above is authoritative.

### Productivity and compounding (chapters 6–7, printed114–155)

Productivity is an opportunity to form new words, with morphological, phonological, semantic and pragmatic constraints. Measuring existing types, tokens and hapaxes answers different questions; corpora and choices of domain affect the answer. Lexical blocking competes with a productive schema but is neither a complete ban on all synonyms nor a substitute for evidence. Compounding requires more than two familiar strings placed beside one another; heads have semantic, categorial and inflectional properties which sometimes diverge. Compound relations may be specified or interpreted pragmatically. Original Dutch learning task: compare fietsbel, deurbel, schoolbel, beltoon, and ask which member names the general category and what relationship the first member adds.

### Paradigms (chapter8, printed156–188)

A paradigm is a set of grammatical cells and their realizations, not simply a list of distinct spellings. Inflection classes group patterns, while grammatical gender concerns agreement; their relationship is real but not identity. An inheritance hierarchy captures shared defaults, with lower classes overriding some details. Stem choice can depend on another paradigm cell even when the meanings are not nested (Priscianic formation). Systematic syncretism differs from accidental homonymy: examine recurrence across classes, syntax, historical analogy and natural classes, rather than any coincidentally identical pair. Defectiveness, deponency and periphrasis show why one-form/one-meaning is not universal. Keep these as later explanation after a learner can fill and interpret Dutch paradigms; show ik loop/jij loopt/wij lopen with differing person/number even when some cells share strings.

### Words, phrases and clitics (chapter9, printed189–210)

Spelling spaces alone do not define the linguistic word. Compare evidence from cohesion, expandability, stress, inflectional domain and syntax; the tests are language-specific and can disagree. Idiomatic meaning is neither necessary nor sufficient for a compound; a phrase can be idiomatic and a compound transparent. Generic dependent nouns are typical, not an exceptionless law. Clitics show prosodic dependence but often broader host selection and word-like syntactic distribution; their properties vary. Lexical integrity is a hypothesis with competing analyses and counterexamples, so do not teach that morphology/syntax are impermeably separate. Dutch separable verbs provide a valuable interface lesson; ensure finite verb movement and lexical verb identity are distinct.

### Form, sound and history (chapter10, printed211–233)

Automatic sound alternations have phonological conditioning; morphophonological alternations require morphological or lexical information as well. Phonetic proximity, distribution in simple words, loanwords, speech styles and boundary domains offer evidence, not one magic test. Relic/common/productive alternations show that many examples do not by themselves prove a live rule. Historical explanations are different from a present speaker's analysis; a historical sound process can be reinterpreted as a grammatical signal. Competing level-ordering and morphology accounts have empirical limitations. For Dutch core lessons, teach spelling, sound and morpheme boundaries as distinct questions with hond/honden and diminutive variants; do not use unreadable IPA details as assessed content.

### Valence and meaning (chapter11, printed234–264)

Semantic roles and syntactic functions are independent dimensions: subjects may be agents, experiencers, stimuli or patients. Passive changes how participants are expressed without deleting the agent from the event; anticausative and stative readings can change event structure. Causatives add a causer. Applicatives may reorganize expression or add a participant, so sharp semantic/syntactic dichotomies can fail. Incorporation and compounds interact with arguments differently across languages. Nominalizations can preserve participant relations while changing their syntactic expression; degree of inheritance depends on construction and reading. Source English judgments on event nouns are not imported as universal Dutch restrictions. Original Dutch transfer: 'De onderzoeker controleert de gegevens' -> 'de controle van de gegevens door de onderzoeker'; identify same participants, changed syntax.

### Frequency (chapter12, printed265–280)

Frequency affects predictability, lexical access, memory and historical change. Frequent values tend to have shorter exponents but richer differentiation; frequent lexemes can preserve irregular forms and undergo phonetic reduction. These are tendencies with counterexamples and local reversals, not a proof that short forms are always common or common forms always irregular. Corpus genres and counting unit matter. Historical frequency examples about profession/gender reflect social usage in their period and should not be taught as current natural laws. Core Dutch application: explain why forms such as ben/is/was are learned as a paradigm rather than forced into a suffix-only analysis; later let learners question a frequency claim with a small explicitly scoped corpus.


## Source cautions checked before Dutch adaptation

Haspelmath/Sims glossary reverses the hyponym direction in its semantic-head wording: the compound is a subtype of its head, not the other way around. The chapter text establishes the intended relationship. Its French periphrastic-future discussion concerns the aller construction, not absence of every one-word French future. A short glossary definition of adjunct as optional must not be used as a sufficient test, since arguments can remain implicit. Some English wordhood and nominalization judgments are specific to the examples and theoretical account. Native Dutch patterns and converging evidence govern app exercises.

## Intended original sixteen-lesson sequence

1. basis-woord: distinguish written word, sound and meaning; discovery with unfamiliar transparent compound.
2. basis-lexeem: occurrence, form, lexeme and family; count tokens/types then group forms.
3. basis-woordsoorten: distribution and inflection as evidence; category separated from sentence function.
4. basis-vorm-functie: semantic role, category and syntactic function; subject need not act.
5. woordbouw-morfemen: systematic segmentation with contrasts; syllable ≠ morpheme.
6. woordbouw-bases-affixen: root/base/stem and input/output; affix selection and scope.
7. woordbouw-buiging-afleiding: grammatical forms versus new lexemes; multiple diagnostics.
8. woordbouw-samenstellingen: head, modifier and semantic relations; composition and lexicalization.
9. woordbouw-hierarchie: brackets and attachment order; justify ambiguity through context.
10. woordbouw-allomorfen: one function across varied forms; sound/spelling/lexical conditions.
11. woordbouw-paradigma: cells, shared forms and irregular stems; infer unknown cells.
12. woordbouw-werkwoordvormen: finite form, infinitive and participle; auxiliaries and tense/aspect distinctions.
13. woordbouw-productiviteit: possible and actual words, domain and frequency measures.
14. woordbouw-conversie: category shift without derivational ending; context and competing analyses.
15. woordbouw-partikels: separable verbs and reduced forms at syntax/morphology interface.
16. woordbouw-onderzoek: collect contrastive evidence, propose/revise rule and qualify conclusion.

Each lesson will progress from a question to three substantial explanations, a manipulation/classification/comparison lab, five distinct practice scenarios with feedback, two later retrieval scenarios, and an open transfer task assessed against explicit checks. Prerequisites follow the sequence with cross-links where needed.
