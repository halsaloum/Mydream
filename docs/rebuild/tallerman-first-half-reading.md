## Cumulative chapter notes and Dutch teaching implications

- Ch.1 §§1.1–1.3 / printed pp.1–26: syntax concerns hierarchical structure, word classes, constituency and relationships, rather than social prescriptions alone. Native judgments and corpora are data; dialect variation does not imply lack of grammar. Relatedness is not necessary for similar structures; unrelated languages can illustrate the same dependency. Grammar changes and languages represent tense/person/number differently. Glosses distinguish independent words, bound affixes and grammatical features; translation alone conceals source structure. A novice Dutch course should start from comparing sentences that work and explaining a manipulation, then introduce terms. Avoid equating 'the first word' with subject or treating word order as the sole syntactic signal across languages.
- Ch.1 exercises / printed pp.27–32: useful pedagogical pattern is discover a recurring distribution/morphological contrast, state the generalization, test new data and distinguish that pattern from an unrelated feature. Genitive/possessive marking expresses more than ownership; domain examples should include a book's cover or tomorrow's plan as relations, not simply a person owning a thing.
- Ch.2 §2.1 / printed pp.33–40: semantic school definitions of noun/verb/adjective are helpful prototypes but fail for abstract nouns, noun modifiers and stative verbs. Use morphosyntax, distribution, grammatical features and function together. A form may belong to several categories in different uses. Dutch example: *Het drinken staat klaar* vs. *Wij drinken water*: same shape, different distribution. Avoid labeling every describing word as adjective or every action-related concept as verb.
- Ch.2 §2.2 / printed pp.40–48: verbs select core participants; one long or coordinated NP remains one syntactic argument. Transitivity is not determined by whether an object immediately follows the verb: displacement does not erase the relationship. Some verbs alternate frames; some require a complement. Tense, aspect, mood, valency and agreement are distinct dimensions. Many languages encode source/person/object via verb morphology; Dutch-specific rules should be separated from typological generalizations.
- Ch.2 §2.3 / printed pp.48–58: semantic roles (agent, experiencer, stimulus, theme/patient, recipient, instrument) differ from grammatical relations (subject/object). Subjects are not always deliberate actors; agreement and pronoun forms supplement position. Full noun phrase boundaries matter. Predication may be nonverbal (noun/adjective). Noun classes/gender are grammatical, not biological in general. Definiteness and determination can be words or morphology and languages vary. The determiner-phrase analysis is a theoretical alternative; keep the beginner Dutch terminology stable while making advanced assumptions explicit.
- Ch.2 §§2.4–2.7 / printed pp.58–70 and exercises pp.70–78: attributive/predicative functions differ; some adjectives are limited to one environment or nongradable. Adjective inflection/agreement and comparison are separate. An adverb is a word class; an adverbial/adjunct is a function that an NP, PP or clause can also fulfill. Do not equate 'says when' with 'is an adverb'. Tallerman's wider adposition analysis treats some English location words/particles as intransitive prepositions, unlike traditional school terminology. For a Dutch novice app, explicitly distinguish the adopted pedagogical analysis from advanced theoretical alternatives rather than silently changing labels.
- Ch.3 §§3.1–3.2 / printed pp.79–90: lexical verb and auxiliary can share forms while performing different work. Finite/nonfinite distinction is distributional; the same surface form can occur in both settings. Participles, infinitives, finite tense and tense reference are distinct. A clause can be imperative, interrogative or a nonverbal predicate; subject can be understood. Coordination links clauses of equal syntactic rank, whereas embedded clauses form hierarchy. Dutch lessons must not import English modal restrictions (*kan, kunnen, kon* inflect) or English do-support.
- Ch.3 §3.3 / printed pp.90–101: matrix and main/root are different labels. A clause can be subordinate to one clause while selecting its own complement; only the topmost clause is root. Complement clauses fill selected arguments; adjunct clauses optionally modify. Relative clauses modify nominals. Complementizer choice and finiteness show dependency. In a Dutch sentence, different finite/main/subordinate ordering is a stronger relevant diagnostic than English tags. Clausal subjects are also possible; not all subjects are a person or noun phrase.
- Ch.3 §3.4 / printed pp.101–107: coordination, nominalization and serial predicates offer alternatives to English-style complementation. Verbal serialization is not merely two neighboring verbs: one clause, shared subject, negation and temporal/mood restrictions, no predicate-argument relation between the verbs. Even the simplified 'one lexical verb = one clause' beginner heuristic has cross-linguistic limits; don't turn it into an absolute universal rule.
- Ch.3 exercises / printed pp.108–118 so far: arguments should be based on positive and negative distribution evidence; genuine category ambiguity or dialect variation can require qualified answers. Morphological agreement in coordinated subjects can target all conjuncts or a nearby conjunct depending on language and word order. Keep Dutch plural agreement training independent from those typological patterns.

- Ch.3 exercises / printed pp.119–121: distributional classification needs to account for both permitted and excluded environments. A label should explain a contrast rather than simply restate the gloss. These exercises reinforce the difference between the form of a clause and its function within a larger sentence.
- Ch.4 / printed pp.122–150: a head determines a phrase's category and selects its complements; its position in the string need not be the first or last word. A head may itself depend on a higher head. Head-initial/head-final order concerns the head and its complements; adjunct position can follow other patterns, and languages can have mixed headedness. Learners should identify the selected relationship before generalizing from word order.
- Ch.4 complement/adjunct evidence: omission is helpful but insufficient. Some complements are optional; noun and adjective complements can be selected even when the resulting short phrase remains grammatical. Conversely a time NP can be an adjunct, while an adverb phrase can fill a selected slot. Category and function therefore require separate questions. A verb's frames may differ across meanings or alternations: an omitted object, an intransitive use, and a new verb sense should not automatically receive the same explanation. Selected prepositions provide useful evidence, supplemented by meaning and distribution.
- Ch.4 head/dependent marking: case on a dependent and agreement on a head are different ways to express a relationship. The same relation may have head marking, dependent marking, double marking, or no overt marking. The core comparison contexts are adposition plus NP, verb plus arguments, a possessed noun plus possessor, and noun plus adjective. Marking location is independent from head position. Dutch morphology and order need their own evidence; a typological example must not become a rule that every language follows.
- Ch.4 exercises / printed pp.151–158: fully read all data and tasks. They ask learners to infer heads, complements, argument frames and marking from distributions and glosses rather than translations alone. Several tables have OCR alignment or symbol errors; app examples should use independently verified Dutch sentences rather than recreate a corrupted source table.
- Ch.5 §5.1 / printed pp.159–171: a string's constituency is specific to its sentence and reading. The same adjacent words can form a constituent in one sentence and cross a boundary in another. Lexical and structural ambiguity are different. Question-answer fragments, echo questions, cleft focus, pronoun replacement and VP proforms offer evidence, but each test has constructional and language-specific constraints. The question or substitute must itself be grammatical. A failed test alone is insufficient where the construction excludes that kind of phrase for independent reasons.
- Ch.5 constituency and displacement: a moved phrase keeps its underlying argument relationship; its surface position does not create an additional object or remove the selected participant. Trees encode hypotheses supported by contrasts; they do not supply evidence merely because they look plausible. A triangle can deliberately suppress internal detail. Nested phrases and adjunct recursion explain why replacing a larger VP may work while replacing an arbitrary substring does not. Learners should compare successful and unsuccessful manipulations and select the smallest supported boundary.
- Ch.5 §5.2 / printed pp.172–173: labelled brackets and trees express the same grouping at a chosen level of detail. Lexical items are the smallest constituents in these trees. A mother immediately dominates its daughters; sisters share a mother. Dominance permits an uninterrupted path through intervening nodes; immediate dominance does not. Category XP follows the head X. S/TP naming reflects theoretical analysis, so novice labels should not be switched silently.
