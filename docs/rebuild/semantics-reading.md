## Cumulative concepts and learning implications

- Ch.1 (printed pp.3–13): meanings belong to words, compositional sentences, and utterances in context. An utterance adds intended action and inferential content. Form and meaning are conventional; idioms are learned as units. Beginners should test one sentence in two believable contexts rather than memorize an abstract semantics/pragmatics split. Keep use/mention separate when describing language.
- Ch.2 (printed pp.15–34): sense differs from denotation/referent. A shift in speaker for *ik/hier/nu* changes reference, not dictionary sense. Indefinites can be specific or nonspecific; quantifier phrases do not simply name a group. Distinguish lexical, structural and referential ambiguity. Emotional/register contributions can persist under negation and differ from descriptive content. Diminutives may express size, affection or minimization. Dutch lesson example: *De begeleider zag de leerling met de verrekijker* needs two groupings and two scenes.
- Ch.3 (printed pp.35–52): knowing truth conditions is not knowing facts. Entailment is a meaning-based guarantee; test asserted premise plus denied consequence, while fixing reference and context. Synonymy is mutual entailment; contraries may both be false; contradictories have opposite truth values. Presuppositions often persist in questions and negation. Triggers encode information as common ground even when it is newly accommodated. Failure can make an utterance inappropriate and sometimes block a truth value; avoid presenting all presupposition as a simple Boolean truth rule. Dutch contrast: *Mila is gestopt met fietsen* says no current cycling and backgrounds earlier cycling; negation removes the first inference but commonly leaves the second.
- Ch.4 begun (printed pp.53–60): logic is a precise metalanguage, not a complete replacement for natural-language meanings. A valid pattern guarantees a true conclusion only when premises are true. Vocabulary entailments differ from proposition-operator and quantifier inferences. Natural *en/of/als* must be revisited as meanings in context rather than automatically equated to logical operators.
- Ch.4 completed (printed pp.61–75): scope, binding and predicate arguments are central; *Niet alle kinderen slapen* differs from *Alle kinderen slapen niet*. Quantifiers are interpreted within a relevant domain. Optional symbolic notation should follow concrete worlds and counterexamples. Do not rebuild logic/fallacy lessons in the protected argumentation course.
- Ch.5 (printed pp.79–106): ambiguity is different from vagueness and underspecification. A variable threshold (*duur*) is not necessarily a new sense; an unspecified characteristic (*docent* gives no age) is not ambiguity. Zeugma, ellipsis identity and contradiction diagnostics reveal distinct senses. Polysemy/homonymy distinctions are sometimes uncertain; origin alone does not establish modern meaning. Figurative coercion creates context-dependent senses; recurring uses can become conventional. Some physical/information aspects of *boek* can coexist without a clash. Curriculum should require a scenario where competing readings give different truth results, and accept reasoned uncertainty at genuine boundaries.
- Ch.6 (printed pp.107–118): sense relations are diagnosed in sentences. Distinguish complementary (*aan/uit*), scalar (*heet/koud*), converse (*lenen aan/lenen van*), reverse (*vullen/legen*), kind-of, and part-of relations. Negation reverses some generic/specific entailments. Same reference is not synonymy; synonym choice can differ by collocation, register or idiom. Learners build a small relation network, then write a discriminating definition.
- Ch.7 (printed pp.119–135): lexical consequences and selectional constraints differ from ordinary world knowledge and conventional collocation. Feature matrices work in constrained domains but fail for all words and require structure/order for relational meanings. Verb classes connect semantics and syntax: contact vs. caused change, causative/inchoative contrasts, argument realization. For Dutch, validate alternations independently rather than importing English grammar wholesale. Use *Sam brak het glas / Het glas brak* alongside *Sam sloeg het glas / Het glas sloeg* and a result test. Do not define semantic classes using only the alternations they are meant to explain.

- Ch.8 (printed pp.139–160): implicatures are calculated from stated meaning, conversation purpose, shared knowledge and contextual assumptions. Maxims are descriptive expectations, not moral rules. Cancellation, reinforcement and sentence-family tests should converge; any one diagnostic can mislead. Ordinary world knowledge differs from linguistic inference. Presuppositions may be suspended or canceled with marked metalinguistic negation; intonation and context matter. Original Dutch pattern: *Hebben alle groepen geoefend? — Sommige groepen wel* usually suggests not all, but *sommige, misschien zelfs alle* shows that suggestion is not an entailment.
- Ch.9 (printed pp.161–177): natural-language operators need contextual interpretation. Time order in *en* and exclusive *of* readings often arise inferentially. Contextual enrichment can help determine truth conditions (*klaar waarvoor?*, *genoeg voor wat?*). The semantics/pragmatics boundary is disputed; do not teach a false clean division. Numerals vary between exact and lower-bound readings in context. Show different task settings (booking exactly three seats vs. a minimum of three participants) before asking how *drie* is interpreted.
- Ch.10 (printed pp.179–196): grammatical form differs from intended speech act. Promises, requests and declarations have conditions for appropriate performance; both literal question and intended request can remain available. Some indirect requests are conventional in usage yet explainable by inference, and paraphrases need not preserve them. Cultural expectations vary; indirectness is not a universal synonym of politeness. Dutch scenario: *Kun je het raam dichtdoen?* in a classroom commonly requests closure; during a movement assessment it can query ability. Have learners identify speaker/addressee/action before rewriting for a new relationship.
- Ch.11 (printed pp.197–213): not all conventional meaning is at issue. Contrast, expressive attitude, sentence adverbs, honorifics and discourse particles can change appropriateness without changing the described event. The same adverb may describe the current speaking act or the manner of a reported action. Dutch *u/je*, *helaas*, *toch*, *even*, *nou* belong in context-rich comparison; do not simply delete them as filler. Cases from Japanese/Korean/German illustrate a general distinction, not a Dutch morphology claim.
- Ch.12 begun (printed pp.217–225): sentence composition tracks constituents, not just word lists. Referent-plus-property and adjective-noun intersection are accessible starting models. Belief/desire complements are opaque: equivalent reference does not guarantee interchangeable belief reports. A specific person vs. whoever satisfies a description creates de re/de dicto contrasts. Example: *Noor zoekt een Nederlandse gids* may name a particular missing person or a required type for a trip.

- Ch.12 completed (printed pp.226–227): specific/nonspecific ambiguity has distinct truth conditions and often interacts with scope, but is not reducible to scope in every analysis.
- Ch.13 (printed pp.229–252): a model fixes the individuals and basic predicate denotations, so composition can be tested separately from ambiguity and incomplete world knowledge. Sets, relations, functions, intersection, union and complement support stepwise interpretation of subject, transitive VP and adjective-noun constituents. For the app, use small visible worlds with people and actions; let learners build or repair a world to make a sentence true. Advanced notation is optional and follows the visible scene.
- Ch.14 (printed pp.253–271): quantifiers relate a restriction set to a scope set. Cardinal and proportional claims differ; *De meeste fietsers dragen een helm* does not mean most helmet wearers are cyclists. *Veel/weinig* are context-dependent and can be cardinal or proportional. Domain is essential. Quantifier, negation, modal and belief scope yield different truth conditions; compare two scenes with the same wording. Preserve existing argumentation quantifier lessons and add linguistic scope work only in the new language course.
- Ch.15 (printed pp.273–290): adjective composition is not always intersection. *rode fiets* intersects; *bekwame arts* depends on being skillful as a doctor; *voormalige docent* does not assert current teacher status; *vermeende winnaar* leaves victory open. Nonintersective does not mean noncompositional. The intension/extension distinction also matters for tense, possible situations, desires and belief. Lambda functions are a formal optional extension, not necessary prerequisite for Dutch beginners.
- Ch.16 (printed pp.293–316): distinguish modal strength (possible/necessary) and type (knowledge, permission/obligation, ability, goal, desire). Context chooses relevant possible situations and standards. Inferential *moet wel thuis zijn* is not direct observation. Scope with negation is language/form-specific: teach Dutch contrasts independently. *Je hoeft niet te vertrekken* removes obligation; *Je mag niet vertrekken* prohibits. Epistemic modality can contribute at-issue content, unlike evaluative adverbs; do not label all speaker-related meaning as nontruthconditional.
- Ch.17 (printed pp.317–328): source of information is distinct from degree of certainty. The correlation can be broken by high-confidence hearsay or uncertain observation. Languages encode evidential categories differently; some modify the speaking act, others the proposition. Dutch lessons should compare lexical source marking (*volgens Noor*, *ik zag dat*, *blijkbaar*) with certainty, avoiding a claim that Dutch has obligatory grammatical evidential suffixes.

- Ch.18 (printed pp.329–345): causal connective meaning operates in described events or in reasons for an assertion/question. *De straat is nat doordat het regende* describes event causality; *Het zal geregend hebben, want de straat is nat* explains evidence for an inference. Negation can target an action, a reason or their relation. Integration/subordination differs from coordination; positions, pause and scope support analysis. Treat English/German punctuation-specific patterns as evidence, then use Dutch *omdat/doordat/want* examples consistent with Dutch word order.
- Ch.19 (printed pp.347–375): ordinary conditionals do not simply equal material implication. Antecedents restrict relevant situations; consequence can be a statement, question or instruction. Standard, relevance, factual and concessive uses carry different commitments. Past morphology can express remoteness rather than past time. Counterfactual interpretation depends on which background facts are kept stable. Dutch word order can distinguish integrated conditions from a prefaced speech-act qualification. A language course should teach form/interpretation/rewriting; preserve deductive-validity and fallacy training in the existing argument course.
- Ch.20 (printed pp.379–403): situation type (state/activity/accomplishment/achievement/single punctual event) differs from grammatical viewpoint. Duration, endpoint and change are separate properties. Objects and paths change telicity: *fietsen* versus *naar school fietsen*. Topic time differs from situation runtime. Ongoing viewpoint does not guarantee completion: *Zij was een brug aan het tekenen* can be true without a completed bridge. Selectional clash can coerce repeated or preparatory readings. Source typo at printed p.393 swaps temporary/permanent labels compared with p.385; use standard stage-level=temporary and individual-level=characteristic/permanent if mentioned.
- Ch.21 (printed pp.405–425): tense relates topic time to speech time or a contextually determined perspective time, not a guarantee that a past state has ended. *De gids was vriendelijk* says something about a past interval without asserting present unfriendliness. Present forms can describe habits, scheduled future or vivid narrative. The English requirement for progressive with current events is not a Dutch rule: *Ik schrijf nu* is ordinary Dutch. Reported speech shifts deictic perspective; tense sequencing varies by language and should not be imported mechanically. Teach timelines and the time the writer is talking about.
- Ch.22 (printed pp.427–452): perfect and perfective are distinct. Experiential, persistent, resulting-state and recent-news readings have different constraints. English present-perfect time-adverb restrictions are not Dutch restrictions: *Ik heb gisteren gewerkt* is grammatical Dutch. Different languages encode or exclude different readings (Baraïn continuation vs. Mandarin discontinuity), so a universal gloss does not establish Dutch behavior. General teaching payoff: learners inspect whether an event happened, continues, has a current result, or precedes another reference point.

## Lesson interaction contract

Each lesson should contain a diagnostic discovery choice, three or more substantial theory sections, analyzed examples that compare minimally different sentences, an executable manipulation, at least five varied independent questions, one transfer writing task with a clear self-assessment rubric, a recap and source anchors. Discovery feedback explains the intuition and the test, rather than only naming a term. Every wrong option in independent practice needs local explanatory feedback addressing its precise misconception.

A useful learning loop is: **predict → inspect a scene or short dialogue → change one word/grouping/context detail → compare → explain the criterion → apply in a fresh text**. Do not mark open writing automatically correct merely because the learner typed enough words. Ask for rubric checking against a revealed model. Do not require term memorization before the learner has seen the contrast. Do not over-score interpretive cases with several plausible readings; provide the relevant context or accept the alternatives with their conditions.

### Original fully worked example: scope

Input: *Iedere leerling leest een verslag.* Scene A: each learner is holding a different report. Scene B: all learners share the same report. The first sentence permits both scenes. To require B, write *Alle leerlingen lezen hetzelfde verslag.* To allow separate choices explicitly, write *Iedere leerling kiest een eigen verslag en leest dat.* The learner should identify that *een verslag* need not denote one globally shared report, and that syntactic word order alone does not choose the interpretation. Practice should ask for a distinguishing world and a disambiguating rewrite, not only the label *bereik*.

### Original fully worked example: intended request

Context: Noor sits beside an open window in a cold classroom. She says *Kun je het raam dichtdoen?* Literally she asks about ability; in this context she asks for the action. A helpful response closes the window or explains why that is impossible. Change the context to an accessibility assessment with an injured arm: the same wording can genuinely ask about ability. The task should allow the learner to compare these contexts, identify the speech act, and rewrite the classroom request with a respectful direct form: *Doe het raam even dicht, alsjeblieft.* Indirectness depends on convention and relationship, so a long indirect sentence is not automatically more considerate.

### Original fully worked example: endpoint

Input A: *Ravi fietste een halfuur.* Input B: *Ravi fietste in een halfuur naar het station.* A specifies duration without a destination; B measures the event by reaching a destination. Input C: *Ravi was naar het station aan het fietsen, toen zijn band lek raakte.* C does not assert that Ravi arrived. A timeline manipulation should make arrival optional for C and necessary for B. The learner then writes an update that says only what the scene supports, preventing the common confusion between starting/in-progress and completion.
